Where AI touches a 500-year studio

Bounded question: How might graduate students studying sustainable design use algorithmic volatility modeling to stress-test anti-fragile systems while upholding human-only stewardship during 500-year scenario planning?

Where does AI touch this course?
One place — Step 2. A volatility model proposes compound climate shocks. It stays out of Steps 1 and 4.
Who does it touch?
The Research Lead runs it and verifies it, the Student Team decides what to keep, and the Course Faculty can strike any scenario.

Make the risk visible

Documented concern

Graduate students push back on ethical grounds when AI enters the teaching workflow. evidence: stakeholder snapshot

Possible benefit

The model holds more interacting variables than a team can at once — permafrost thaw × acidification × biome lag across centuries — so stress tests reach compound failures, not single shocks. assumption

Preliminary safeguard

Two-paper rule: no AI-proposed shock enters the Stress-Test Record until the Research Lead ties it to two peer-reviewed empirical climate papers. Every prompt and output goes in an AI-use log that faculty read at the Step 3 critique.

Unresolved question to test

Does the model's menu of shocks narrow what teams imagine? Test: one team drafts shocks by hand before seeing any model output, then compare the range and novelty of both lists. proposed test

    Stress-test chat

    ◆ Step 2 · the AI insertion point

    Describe your system or a resilience hypothesis. The AI suggests candidate 500-year shocks for you to test. Each one is a hypothesis: the Research Lead still has to back it with 2 peer-reviewed papers, and your team decides what to simulate.

    Example: "Our system is a coastal wetland food network in the Savannah River delta. We think the rice-and-oyster cycle is anti-fragile to sea-level rise." Tell me about yours.

    Transparency log: Stress-test chat

    Status: does not meet the Step 2 goal

    Across multiple runs, the chat returns research directions in words. It does not model volatility, and it never processes numbers or runs a scenario. The course asks for quantitative stress-testing, so on its own this tool can't complete Step 2. evidence: repeated test runs

    What the tool actually does
    • Reads the student's text and writes back up to 3 candidate shocks in prose
    • Describes a possible cascade for each shock in words
    • Suggests the kind of peer-reviewed papers to look for
    • Asks one question back about the team's hypothesis
    • Runs on a free language model (OpenCode Zen), so answers change from run to run
    What it was supposed to do
    • Algorithmic volatility modeling: simulate compounding 500-year shocks
    • Put numbers on each shock: probability, magnitude, timing, rate of change
    • Process a scenario through the team's system and show where it fails
    • Fill the Four-Quadrant Volatility Matrix and the Stress-Test Record
    What it doesn't do
    • No calculations, simulations, probabilities or data of any kind
    • No access to climate datasets or models
    • Can't read or check papers, so its research suggestions are unverified
    • Doesn't give the same answer twice, so no result is reproducible
    • Keeps no record; the log below is kept only in this browser
    • Can't tell whether a shock is realistic; it only sounds plausible
    What the human has to do
    • Research Lead: find and read 2 peer-reviewed papers for every shock before it's used
    • Student Team: do the quantitative modeling in a real tool (a spreadsheet, system-dynamics software or published climate projections)
    • Student Team: choose which shock to simulate and fill the Volatility Matrix by hand
    • Student Team: copy every AI output they use, with the run number, into the Stress-Test Record
    • Course Faculty: review the log at the Step 3 critique and strike anything unverified
